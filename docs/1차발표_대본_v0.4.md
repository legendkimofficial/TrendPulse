## 1. Title  ⏱ 0:00 → 0:15

> Hello everyone. We are team cloudnine, and this is **Trend Pulse**.
> In one sentence: Trend Pulse reads a stream of news headlines and tells you what the news is talking about right now, **without a list of keywords**.

---

## 2. What is the news talking about right now?  ⏱ 0:15 → 0:50

> There are two easy ways to find hot topics.
> One: write a list of keywords and count them. Simple, but somebody has to write the list. Nobody had the word "scalia" on a list before Justice Scalia died.
> Two: count every word, forever. That finds everything, but the memory **never stops growing**.
> So we want three things: new topics with no list, a small memory, and a clear picture of **what we lose** by keeping it small.

---

## 3. How a word gets its score  ⏱ 0:50 → 1:35

> This is how it works. It is the exponentially decaying window from week four.
> `[1번 상자]` When a headline arrives, we split it into words and two-word phrases, like "supreme court".
> `[2번]` Every day, all scores shrink a little. After seven days a score is **half** of what it was.
> `[3번]` Words in the new headline get one point.
> `[4번]` The important step is the last one. When a score falls below 0.5, we **delete** the word. That is where we save memory.
> `[왼쪽 아래 상자]` And we can make a promise: a deleted word loses **at most one point**.
> `[오른쪽 아래]` To check this, we also run an exact version that never deletes anything. It is only there to measure our errors.

---

## 4. Ten years of HuffPost headlines  ⏱ 1:35 → 2:00

> Our data is about two hundred thousand HuffPost headlines, from 2012 to 2022. We replay them in date order, one day at a time.
> Two things to remember. The data has dates but no time of day, so this is a **replay**, not live news.
> `[타임라인 가리키며]` And after 2018, HuffPost publishes only about **five headlines a day**. This matters later.

---

## 5. February 16, 2016  ⏱ 2:00 → 2:50

> Let me show the dashboard. This is February 16, 2016, three days after Justice Scalia died.
> On the left is the Top 20. "scalia" is at rank 12, and "supreme court" is at rank 8. **Nobody told the system about this event.**
> `[≈ 표시 가리키며]` Now look at the small "almost equal" sign next to "gop" and "scalia". It means the two scores are so close that saving memory **may have swapped them**. And it did: the exact ranks are 12 and 11. `[선택]` We do not hide our errors; we mark them.
> `[오른쪽]` On the right are the **bursts**: words growing much faster than usual. "scalia" is growing two times faster than normal.

## 6. What do we lose by saving memory?  ⏱ 2:50 → 3:30

> So, what do we lose by saving memory? Left is our ranking, right is the exact version.
> We store **93 percent fewer keywords**.
> Still, **99.9 percent** of the time, the Top 20 has the same members as the exact version.
> The order is exactly the same in **89 percent** of the snapshots. The other 11 percent are small swaps between neighbours with almost equal scores, like gop and scalia.
> We mark every one of them. `[선택]` In about 38 thousand pairs, we missed none.

> Does it find real events? We took 26 real events, like Hurricane Sandy, Brexit and the Notre-Dame fire, and asked: does the word enter the Top 20 within seven days?

## Likely questions (English answers, short)

**Q1. Did you really save 93 percent of the memory?**
> 93 percent is the number of stored keywords. The real Python object size went down 84 percent, because our version also keeps a small heap to find words to delete. Article text and the search index are not included in either number.

**Q2. Why count by day instead of by article? Why not from the start?**
> From 2012 to 2017 both give almost the same result, so we did not see the difference. After 2018 there are only five headlines a day, so "the last 1,000 articles" means months. Counting by day fixed that, but it also lets words with only one or two headlines into the Top 20. That is the thin-evidence problem on slide 8.

**Q3. You chose the 26 events yourselves. Is that fair?**
> Partly. The first 16 events were chosen earlier, and we added extra expressions after seeing results, so that part is post-hoc. The last 10 events were written down before we ran this round, with their dates and expressions. For those 10, the result is 3 out of 10 versus 9 out of 10. A test on random dates is on our list.

**Q4. How do you know the "almost equal" mark catches every swap?**
> The exact score is always between the shown score and the shown score plus a bound. We mark two neighbours only if the lower one could still pass the upper one. That is a mathematical guarantee for the raw scores, and we also checked about 38 thousand neighbour pairs on real data and found no missed swap.

**Q5. What is DGIM doing in this project?**
> DGIM is a helper, not the main ranking. For a keyword you pick, it estimates how many of the last N articles contained it, with a known error bound. The ranking itself comes from the decaying window.

**Q6. Can the explaining agent make things up?**
> It must call our tools first, and every article it cites is checked against the articles it actually looked up. If the check fails, the answer is rewritten once, and if it fails again, we drop it and show a plain summary. This flow is tested offline; we have not run it with the real Solar API yet.

**Q7. Why is the approximate version slower at reading articles?**
> Each update also maintains the heap that finds words to delete. In return, ranking is about 20 times faster because there are far fewer words to sort.
